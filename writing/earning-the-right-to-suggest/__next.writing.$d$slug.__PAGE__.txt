21:T5c9,A few years ago, at a large consumer tech company, I watched a designer finish a launch poster for a quick-commerce service. The promise was simple: delivery in under 15 minutes. The poster had a green fast-forward icon to make the point.

Before the stakeholder review, and before our Friday group critique, the designer quietly ran the poster past an AI model. It was a rehearsal, not a replacement. They wanted to walk in prepared.

The feedback was useful. It covered layout, visual hierarchy, and whether the message of speed came through. The designer went into both meetings sharper for it.

But it said nothing about the type.

In the Friday critique, someone spotted it. The icon said fast, but the word “Quick” was set in the brand typeface at regular width, like every other word on the poster. We changed it to a condensed italic. The letters started to move. A human caught the gap between what the poster was saying and how its letters were saying it. The AI hadn't.

Since then I've seen the same habit everywhere. Designers share their work with ChatGPT, Claude and Gemini before a review. They want critique on their own terms, before facing other people. And typography, the part of a design that carries the most emotion, gets almost no specific feedback.

I carried that Friday critique with me when I moved to Monotype. The fourth layer of our plugin, a typographic critic, is my answer to it. But before a system can offer taste, it has to earn the right.22:T407,At Monotype, our Adobe plugin works across four layers of a designer's workflow:

1. Font auto-activation: making sure the fonts in a document are installed and ready.
2. Licensing: surfacing risks such as expired, expiring or overused fonts, or fonts not marked for production.
3. Brand Type Fidelity (BTF): checking type against brand guidelines.
4. Creative advice: a typographic critic and design partner. This one is still a concept.

To be clear, not all of this is agentic. The first two layers are deterministic automation. BTF runs an agentic workflow, and creative advice would be a truly agentic experience. So I'm using “ladder” in the traditional sense, the levels of automation that Sheridan and Verplank described in 1978, running from LOA 1 (the human does everything) to LOA 10 (the system acts alone).

Every layer has to answer the same question: how much should the system do before a human says so?

The answer isn't the same for each layer. 2a:T58f,Licensing is deterministic, and the checks are reliable. By the logic of layer one, it should just run all the time. It doesn't, on purpose.

The problem is who it belongs to. An admin responsible for 50 designers, each working on five documents, can't possibly track which fonts have expired or haven't been marked for production. Admins told us the same thing over and over: this is not my headache, I just need to know we're compliant. Designers say the same thing about licensing, for the opposite reason.

The admin is accountable but can't see anything. The designer can see everything but doesn't care. And we can't tell where a designer is in their process. They could be exploring, or about to ship.

So we only interrupt at moments of commitment. Scans run when a document opens, when the designer saves, or when they ask for one, never on a timer. Production-marking nudges show up when a designer exports a file or outlines a font, the moments when a design becomes real.

The designer doesn't have to fix anything either. From the breach card, one tap notifies the admin that a font is expired, expiring, overused or needs marking for production. Admins also get their own rolled-up views: imported fonts with every document they appear in, and license management showing usage across each Monotype font style.

Exploring is protected. Committing gets checked. 2b:T476,This is the layer that began with that poster. The concept was born in that Friday critique, before I ever joined Monotype.

The creative-advice concept is simple: select a text layer and ask for typographic critique. It would help a designer choose the right font and use it well, in a way that fits the document and the brand guidelines BTF already holds.

This is where the title comes from. A typographic critic with no grounding is just another opinion. This one would stand on everything below it: the fonts the system already knows, the licenses it has checked, the brand rules it has learned. It doesn't earn the right to suggest by being clever. It inherits that right from the layers underneath.

It will also always be user-initiated, never proactive. Taste is the most subjective call in the workflow, and unasked-for taste is where trust breaks fastest. The designers I watched already showed what they wanted: critique they ask for, privately, before facing other people.

On the poster, that's the moment the night before. Select “Quick”, ask, and hear: your icon says speed, but your letters don't.
